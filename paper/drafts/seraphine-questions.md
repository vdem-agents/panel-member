Thanks for your feedback on the proposal. I suppose the short answer to your question is that I felt that the paper should have a social science hook in addition to a V-Dem hook and I have been searching for that balance. But let me elaborate on that a little bit. 

To me the information question, and in particular the data leakage issue inherent in a lot of social science LLM research, is both interesting and unaddressed in the literature. For example, the Benoit et. al. AJPS piece assumes that the manifesto summaries are driving the LLM rating behavior, but those party manifestos, information about the parties themselves, and other related actors are already in the pretraining data. So it could just be that the LLMs are working off of their priors and maybe that also explains why you see so much bias in their ratings relative to human coders, which is what we see evidence of in the recent Weidman et. al. PS piece. 

Furthermore, the best way to answer these questions is experimentally, carefully changing one input at a time, like the information or the model or the context, and seeing what kind of effect it has, as opposed to just showing a correlation between LLM and human coder scores (as is commonly done in political science). So my idea was to test whether the base Llama model or the three fine-tuned conditions make a difference in terms of the extent to which the models are even registering the textual information provided to them. And then we are adjusting the conditions, e.g. for salient countries (those mentioned in the news a lot), countries undergoing regime transitions, etc. So it is a lot of experiments all aimed toward answering this question of how much is the model relying on the contextual knowledge (what is presented to it in the prompt) versus how much it is relying on parametric knowledge (what it learned during training). 

I agree it would be really cool to set up a more sophisticated model that accesses the web or does some other kinds of tool calling, but as I thought about it more I saw some problems with it. First, we have not answered the basic question of how much is the model actually taking into account new information, and that seems like an important first step. Second, there are a lot of stochastic moving parts that get introduced at the point that we introduce tool calling, like which information gets accessed or, even if we point it to just Wikipedia, what is the level of coverage for various countries--do some of them even have pages related to the indicators we want to look at? Third, since we are working primarily with historical data in V-Dem, how do you access the information relevant to the question a decade ago as opposed to today's data (even a year or two ago might be a challenge). Finally, you have the issue of replicability--how can others replicate our findings if we use tool calling; how would they know what information the LLM accessed? 

So I do think there could be some very interesting second-order questions that we could answer with a more advanced tool-calling setup but from a social scientific standpoint fixed information packets made more sense. Maybe, for example, it would be cool to explore tool calling for updating scores in real time? But I thought that could perhaps be a second or third paper, and that the information question would be best addressed using fixed information packets. 

As for why Llama 3.3 Instruct 70B. A few reasons. One is that it is open source and I think political scientists should really prioritize that from the standpoint of open science and reproducibility. Another is that it is big enough to be relatively well-informed about the world and engineered to follow instructions (thus the name "Instruct") but small enough to fine-tune and to be able to run it on a university cluster. I wanted to do the larger version as well but it is a real challenge to run let alone fine-tune a  405B model on A100s or even H100s with queues for the clusters. The model is also often used in other similar studies (including the Benoit piece) and is roughly comparable and often outperforms ChatGPT 4o mini, which as you know a lot of PSC studies also use. We could expand to other models for robustness but we get a lot of traction on the information by looking at one model and comparing its behavior across a series of controlled conditions. 

Why use State Department and FH reports? First, I think they do a good job of summarizing changes in the political landscape from year to year. They are also very thorough in terms of the topics that they cover and very consistent in terms of their format, which makes it easy to map the various sections to the 205 indicators that we are looking at. This changes with Trump 2 for the State Department reports, but generally speaking the two sources provide a consistent and predictable source of basic information that we can feed to the LLM for the vast majority of indicators in the analysis. They also go back in time, so unlike Wikipedia or some other web source, we can be assured that the information generating process is the same for 10 years ago as it was in 2023. I did think about expanding to other fixed sources that are mentioned in the post-coder survey, but none of them were as readily available as these two. Of course future experiments could always expand the list of sources to see if that makes a difference! 

You asked about the reasons for the decline in V-Dem panel sizes. I am not sure what the exact reason is, but this is one example of how I could use your help in contextualizing the analysis. As for what the AI's contribution would be in real life, my thinking now would be to establish that at least one of the models we are testing (base or one of the fine-tuned variants) does as well as a human in terms of its distance from the panel mean and show that replacing one human with an AI in a thin panel does not meaningfully change the estimates. If we wanted to expand beyond that, we would need to experiment with temperature of persona prompting to vary the LLMs response within a reasonably bounded range, because obviously we would not want to deploy exactly the same AI multiple times on the same panel. That could be a really cool follow-on paper. 

You made the point that human coders are also vulnerable to their biases, e.g. we also have training data and a "knowledge cut off." The idea is not that LLMs should never rely on pretraining, but that if they are doing that to the extent that they are ignoring text then we would have a problem because the models would not then be able to replicate the behavior of a human expert due to the fact that they are not appropriately updating their priors. Of course, you could also argue that human coders are stubborn as well and do not pay attention to the evidence, but I guess that is going to be a bigger epistemological debate. I would just say that ideally we would probably want either a human or AI coder to fully take into account new information and weigh that against previous learning or, to put it in ML terms, to make out of sample predictions.
