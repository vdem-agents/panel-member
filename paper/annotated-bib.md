# Annotated Bibliography

## CS Studies

**Xu, Rongwu, Zehan Qi, Zhijiang Guo, Cunxiang Wang, Hongru Wang, Yue Zhang, and Wei Xu. 2024. "Knowledge Conflicts for LLMs: A Survey." *Proceedings of EMNLP 2024*, 8541–8565.**

Surveys how large language models behave when their parametric knowledge (learned during pretraining) conflicts with contextual knowledge (supplied at inference time), organizing the literature into three conflict types: context-memory (the case relevant here — provided text contradicts what the model already "knows"), inter-context (conflicting passages within the provided text itself), and intra-memory (the model gives inconsistent answers to differently phrased queries about the same fact, indicating internally conflicting parametric knowledge). Reviews causes of each conflict type, documents how models behave when conflicts arise, and surveys mitigation strategies. Establishes "parametric vs. contextual knowledge" as the standard vocabulary for this question in the NLP literature — a more precise framing than "anchoring bias" for what this study's B1–B4 and R4 hypotheses test, since context-memory conflict is specifically about reliance on stored subject-knowledge over supplied evidence, not the Kahneman-style numeric-priming effect that "anchoring" strictly denotes.

**Longpre, Shayne, Kartik Perisetla, Anthony Chen, Nikhil Ramesh, Chris DuBois, and Sameer Singh. 2021. "Entity-Based Knowledge Conflicts in Question Answering." *Proceedings of EMNLP 2021*, 7052–7063.**

Originates the entity-substitution paradigm for studying knowledge conflicts: named entities in a QA passage (e.g., person, organization, or location mentions) are swapped for other entities of the same type, creating a passage whose content contradicts the model's parametric knowledge about the original entity. Finds that models substantially over-rely on memorized (parametric) information even when the passage in front of them says otherwise — the mechanism behind hallucination in knowledge-dependent tasks — and identifies factors that make over-reliance worse (e.g., higher entity popularity, higher model confidence in the parametric answer). Proposes a training-time mitigation that reduces over-reliance on parametric knowledge and improves out-of-distribution generalization by 4–7%. The entity-substitution method is structurally identical to this study's name-swap test (R4): swap the named country between two evidence packets and check whether the rating follows the name or the text.

**Liu, Nelson F., Kevin Lin, John Hewitt, Ashwin Paranjape, Michele Bevilacqua, Fabio Petroni, and Percy Liang. 2024. "Lost in the Middle: How Language Models Use Long Contexts." *Transactions of the Association for Computational Linguistics* 12: 157–173. doi:10.1162/tacl_a_00638 (arXiv:2307.03172).**

Tests how the *position* of relevant information inside a long input context affects whether a model uses it, on multi-document question answering and key–value retrieval. Finds a U-shaped curve: performance is highest when the relevant passage sits at the beginning or the end of the context and degrades substantially when the model has to reach for it in the middle — a pattern that holds even for models explicitly built for long contexts. Relevant here because the coding prompt places the focal country and year in exactly the two high-salience positions (the opening task statement and the closing output instruction) while names inside the evidence body fall in the middle. This is the main reason the framing-vs-text decomposition cannot be read as evidence that *task instructions* are architecturally privileged: serial position is a competing explanation for the same asymmetry, and the present design does not separate them.

**Xiao, Guangxuan, Yuandong Tian, Beidi Chen, Song Han, and Mike Lewis. 2024. "Efficient Streaming Language Models with Attention Sinks." *ICLR 2024* (arXiv:2309.17453).**

Introduces the "attention sink" phenomenon: transformer language models direct disproportionate attention to the first few tokens of a sequence regardless of whether those tokens carry semantic content, and this behavior is load-bearing enough that preserving the initial tokens' key–value states is what allows a sliding-window model to stay stable over very long streams. Cited here only for the underlying observation — that early context positions receive structurally elevated attention — as mechanistic backing for the primacy half of the position argument. The engineering contribution (StreamingLLM) is not relevant to this paper.

**Zhao, Tony Z., Eric Wallace, Shi Feng, Dan Klein, and Sameer Singh. 2021. "Calibrate Before Use: Improving Few-Shot Performance of Language Models." *Proceedings of ICML 2021*, PMLR 139 (arXiv:2102.09690).**

Documents systematic biases in few-shot prompting, including a *recency bias* whereby the model disproportionately repeats the label of the example appearing last in the prompt, alongside majority-label and common-token biases; proposes contextual calibration using content-free inputs ("N/A") to equalize class scores. Relevant on two axes. First, the recency result is the second leg of the position argument: material nearest the generation point exerts outsized influence, which is where this study's closing "Rate {COUNTRY} in {YEAR}" instruction sits. Second, it is a caution for the calibration block itself — the few-shot examples are ordered, and ordering effects are a live alternative explanation for any few-shot-related contrast.
