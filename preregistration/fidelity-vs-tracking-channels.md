Let me make both halves precise.

Why the design can defend a usage claim at all

"Information usage" is measured through two channels in your design, and they have very different epistemic standing:

The fidelity channel (MAE deltas) is an indirect proxy. It infers usage from improvement against the panel mean, so it inherits every problem you raised — panels aren't objective, and usage-with-divergent-weighting looks identical to non-usage. On its own, this channel supports usage claims only in one direction: a gain under de-identified conditions is hard to explain without text-reading, but a null or a loss is ambiguous among three explanations (no use, anchoring distortion, genuine use that diverges from panel weighting).

The tracking channel (name-swap, re-identification stratification, transition moderation, post-cutoff holdout) measures usage almost directly, and — this is the crucial property — its discriminating logic is insulated from panel validity. The name-swap test asks which of two panel means the rating tracks; any bias shared by both panels cancels out of the comparison. The 2024 holdout doesn't need panels to be right, only stable, because the model structurally cannot have priors about the year. The transition moderation predicts where text helps, which prior-recall cannot mimic. These tests answer "did the model condition on the text?" without ever assuming the panel is truth.

So the honest defense is: the paper identifies information usage because the tracking channel exists, and the fidelity channel then quantifies what that usage is worth for the applied task. A paper that ran only the 4×4 MAE grid would be vulnerable to exactly the objection you articulated. Yours isn't — provided the mechanism tests are framed as the backbone of the usage question rather than as robustness addenda, which is a framing change, not a design change.
